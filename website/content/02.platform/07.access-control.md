## Overview

Every organization's developer-side access lives in the platform's authorization store: who may view, edit or delete each application, project and entity definition, and who may manage the organization's members, machines, integrations and access. A grant gives a user or a group a role on a resource, and the role's permissions reach the resource and everything inside it, so where a grant is made decides what it covers. The console manages all of it through `PermissionService`, published in `management-api` for an organization's members; every function derives the organization from the caller, and a role, a group, a user or a resource it names must belong to that organization. Every request a member makes is checked against these grants where it enters the platform, so a grant is all it takes for a member to act and a revocation all it takes to stop them. An application's end users have a tier of their own, in the application's own store, described below.

The figures on this page share one palette: violet for what is declared in code and derived at registration, and for roles and bindings; amber for the service directory; blue for the request path and its callers; green for the reconciler and the scope objects; teal for the engine and the walk it makes. Each figure opens full screen on a click.

## Permissions

A permission is named for a resource type and an action: `project_can_edit`, `entity_definition_can_delete`, `application_can_view`, `organization_can_manage_members`. The platform's services declare them, so the catalog `findPermissions` returns is whatever the running release publishes, grouped by type; adding a function to a service adds its permission on the next release. The derived permissions imply each other, deleting implies editing and editing implies viewing, and a named permission such as `can_report` stands alone unless its service says otherwise.

The organization's own permissions are what its members manage rather than what they build: `can_view_members` and `can_manage_members` for the members and invitations, `can_view_machines` and `can_manage_machines` for the machines, `can_view_integrations` and `can_manage_integrations` for the GitHub installations, `can_view_access` and `can_manage_access` for the roles, groups and grants, `can_view_telemetry` for the logs, metrics and traces, and `can_view_jobs` for the job runs.

## Roles

A role bundles permissions. The model defines a built-in viewer, editor and admin for every resource type, named `<type>.viewer`, `<type>.editor` and `<type>.admin`: the viewer holds the type's reading permissions, `can_view`, `can_read`, `can_search` and every `can_view_<something>`, the editor everything but deleting, and the admin everything on the type and on everything inside it. `organization.admin` is therefore the organization's administrator, and `application.developer` holds everything inside an application without the application's own permissions. A service may declare roles of its type beside the built-in ones, for a use the three levels do not fit: `vm_node.registrar` bundles the one permission a machine needs to register a node, and `vm_node.agent` the two a registered node reports with. Built-in roles cannot be changed.

The member who creates an organization by signing up is bound as its administrator. An organization a migration seeds, such as the one the end-to-end tests use, records its creator the same way, and the management server binds that creator as the administrator when it starts and finds the organization has none; an organization whose administrator was revoked is left as it is.

A custom role is defined by an organization from the catalog, with `saveRole`: a name, a description and the permissions it bundles. Changing a role's permissions changes every grant of it at once. A role is deleted with `deleteRole` once no grant holds it; a grant is revoked where it was made.

::authorization-diagram{view="roles"}
::

A role is a bundle of permissions and nothing else. The three rings exist for every type of every store; the bars exist in the platform store; the declared ones come from `@AuthzRole` on a service, and an organization's own from `saveRole`. All of them are the same kind of tuple in the engine.

## Groups

A group collects an organization's members so one grant reaches all of them: `saveGroup`, `addGroupMember` and `removeGroupMember` manage it, and a grant made to a group reaches whoever is in the group at the time of a check. A group is deleted with `deleteGroup` once no grant holds it.

## Grants

`grant` gives a user, one of the organization's machines or a group a role on the organization, or on an application, a project or an entity definition inside it; `revoke` takes it back where it was made. The subject holds every permission the role bundles on the resource and on everything inside it:

| Wanted | Grant |
|---|---|
| Sally can modify project A but not B | `project.editor` on project A |
| Marcus can work with every project of Atlas CRM | `application.developer` on the application |
| Priya can work with every project of every application, including ones created later | `application.developer` on the organization |
| QA can view every project of the organization but edit none | `project.viewer` on the organization, with the group as the subject |

`findGrants` lists the grants that reach a resource: those made on it, then those made on each ancestor up to the organization, each naming the resource it was made on, so the console shows an inherited grant with a link to where it was made.

`grant` is one writer of a grant among several. A grant is one binding in a store, and these are the only writers of one, drawn beside the object each binds on, in the platform's store and in an application's:

A teal arrow is a binding written with the role on it; a dashed region is the set of objects an access service may bind any role on, after checking that the role, the subject and the object belong where the caller may grant. ## Reading access

`listAccessible` returns the resources of a type the caller holds a permission on, through any grant that reaches them; it accepts the engine's cache window. The platform's own listings read it before they query: `ApplicationService` lists, counts, searches and finds by id only the applications the caller may see, which are the ones the caller may view and the ones containing a project or an entity definition the caller may view, `ProjectService` lists, counts, searches and finds by id only the projects the caller may view and those containing an entity definition the caller may view, and `EntityDefinitionService` finds by id only a definition the caller may view. A member granted `project.editor` on one project therefore sees that project's application and, inside it, that project alone; an application, a project or an entity definition the member may not see is absent from a listing and answered as not found by id, as an id nothing has is, and everything else the member asks of a hidden project is refused as before. `explain` answers whether a subject holds a permission on a resource and names the grants it holds the permission through: each one a role bundling the permission, made to the subject or to a group the subject is in, on the resource or on an ancestor. It asks the engine for a consistent answer, so it reflects a grant made or revoked a moment ago. A permission held through nothing a grant explains, such as a membership, is allowed with no grant named.

## Enforcement

A request is checked once, where the platform stamps the caller onto it: the STOMP gateway, for a request a client sends, and the MCP endpoint, for a tool call. Both pass the request's destination, the caller, and the body to the same authorizer before anything is sent, and a refusal is answered to the caller as an `AuthorizationException` reading `Not authorized`; a tool call gets it as the tool's error result. The reason, such as the permission and the object the caller was refused, or the id the request's body lacked, is logged at `WARN` by the server that refused it, and a server with `kinotic.debug` on, as the `development` profile sets it, answers `Not authorized: ` followed by the reason. Hops inside the platform carry the caller's participant and are not checked again, so a request that cascades through several services is authorized by the check at its entry. The request's path through the gateway, from the zone rule to the engine and on to the bus, is drawn on [Defense in Depth](/platform/defense-in-depth#request-authorization).

The check is the one the function's definition carries. The authorizer reads the definition of the service a request addresses from the service directory once and keeps it until the directory announces the contract written again, and for five minutes at most, then resolves the object the function's check names from the request, and asks the engine whether the caller holds the permission on it: the object's id is read from the body, at the parameter's position in a client's request or by the parameter's name in a tool call, and down a property path such as `entity.id`; a reference to the caller's scope, such as `{@organizationId}`, is read from the caller. A check on a scope level the caller's scope stops above is made on the caller's own level: an organization member asking for an application's projects, or re-syncing their index, is checked for the permission on the organization, which carries the permissions of everything inside it. A request that names no object its check needs is refused without asking the engine, as is one whose body no id can be read from, and one whose body names a property twice, of which the service would act on an occurrence other than the one checked. An application's or a project's id is unique only within its organization, so the engine knows each as `<organizationId>.<id>`, `application:acme.crm` for the application `crm` of `acme`: the authorizer names an application or a project the request names within the caller's organization, and refuses a caller outside every organization one, while `PermissionService` and `ApplicationAccessService` take and answer resources with the id the organization knows.

A delegate, such as a session the CLI holds for a member, is checked as the member it acts for. A function is served without a check only when its definition marks it unchecked; a request no contract covers, to a service the directory holds no definition for, to a function the definition leaves out or marks neither way, or on a node running no directory, is refused. The zone rules hold before any of this: a client reaches only the zones its participant may address. The platform's own operators and machines are checked like everyone else, on the platform's resources described below, and an application's end users against the application's own store, on the tenant each belongs to.

A server reports itself healthy only once the platform store runs a model, since until then every checked request is refused. An application whose store runs no model yet refuses its end users the same way.

A function's check normally answers from the engine's caches, which may lag a change by a moment. A function declared `consistent`, one where a stale allow would be a security event, such as removing or disabling a member or rotating a machine's secret, is answered from the store itself.

### What one check walks

What the engine does with the one check it is asked is a walk over the tuples the platform wrote. A member of an organization asks for a project, `project:p #project_can_view @user:u` in the platform's store:

::authorization-diagram{view="walk"}
::

Teal edges are the walk for one binding; the badges mark its steps:

1. Read every `role_binding` attached to `project:p`. The cost of a check grows with this count.
2. For each binding, is `user:u` its member? Directly, through a group the user is in, or because the member is a membership userset such as `organization:o#member`.
3. Does the binding's role bundle `project_can_view`? That is the tuple `role:r #project_can_view @user:*`, written for every built-in and declared role when the model is, and for an organization's own role by `saveRole`.
4. No binding matched: try the permissions that imply it, `project_can_edit` and through it `project_can_delete`, the same way.
5. Still nothing: follow `project:p #application` to `application:a` and evaluate `project_can_view` there, then on `organization:o`, then on `platform:kinotic`, each against its own bindings. A platform operator's grant on the platform reaches every project this way.

In an application's store the same walk runs on `tenant:t` for the application's own types and climbs to `application:a`. The tuples it walks, and who writes them:

| Tuple | Written by |
|---|---|
| `organization:o #platform @platform:kinotic`, `application:a #organization @organization:o`, `project:p #application @application:a`, `entity_definition:d #application @application:a` | the lifecycle services, in the platform store, as part of the write that creates each; a definition's containment also in the application's store, at create, removed at delete |
| `organization:o #member @user:u` | the identity service, in the platform store, for an organization's user |
| `application:a #end_user @user:u`, `tenant:t #application @application:a`, `tenant:t #member @user:u` | the same, for an application's user: the end-user tuple in both stores, the tenant's two in the application's |
| `application:a #placed @user:*` | the application's store provisioning, once per store, after the model and its roles |
| `role:r #<permission> @user:*` | the store's worker with the model, for every role the model defines; `saveRole` for an organization's own |
| `role_binding:b #role @role:r`, `role_binding:b #member @…`, `<object> #role_binding @role_binding:b` | one binding per grant, by the writers drawn under [Grants](#grants) |
| `group:g #member @user:u` | `addGroupMember`, in the platform store |

## Declaring what a function does

A declaration becomes an entry in the directory and a relation in the model before any request is checked:

::authorization-diagram{view="directory"}
::

Registration converts the interface once and stores the derived checks with the contract. Nothing is derived at request time; the authorizer reads what registration stored, and the reconciler regenerates the whole model from every stored contract of the store.

A service declares its resource once, with `@AuthzResource`, and the check of each function is derived from the function's name and parameters: a `find`, `get`, `count`, `search` or `list` function needs `can_view`, a `save`, `update` or `set` function `can_edit`, a `delete` or `remove` function `can_delete`, and a `create` function `can_edit` of the type on the parent the argument names. The object is the resource id the function names: a parameter called `id` or `<type>Id`, or the `id` of an argument of the resource type; a function naming none is checked on the parent, read from the argument naming it or from the caller's scope.

| Declaration | What the derivation stores on the function |
|---|---|
| Function name starts with `find`, `get`, `count`, `search`, `list` | permission `can_view` on the service's type |
| `save`, `update`, `set`, `create` | permission `can_edit` |
| `delete`, `remove` | permission `can_delete` |
| Any other verb | registration fails unless `@AuthzCheck(permission = …)` names one |
| A parameter named `id` or `<type>Id`, or an object parameter with an `id` property | resourceId template `{id}`, `{projectId}`, `{entry.id}`; the check is on `type:<value>` |
| No id parameter | the check moves to the parent: `{<parent>Id}` parameter or property, else the caller's scope such as `{@applicationId}`; the permission keeps the type's name |
| `@AuthzCheck(resource, resourceId, permission, implies, consistent)` | each attribute overrides the derived value; `implies` adds permissions the engine treats as granted along with it |
| `@AuthzUnchecked` | the unchecked mark is stored; the function is served without the engine. Beside `@AuthzCheck`, or on a type beside `@AuthzResource`, registration fails; on a type declaring no resource it marks every function |

Every function of a resource service ends up as one check with three parts, settled once at registration. The permission, the type and the id each come from the first rung of a ladder that matches, and a ladder that reaches its end refuses the service:

::authorization-diagram{view="parts"}
::

The highlighted rungs are the ones the example matched; only the id is read again per request.

A function the derivation cannot read is declared with `@AuthzCheck`: `permission` names a permission of its own, such as `can_deploy` or `can_view_members`, `resource` names another type, a constant, and `resourceId` the id of the resource checked, a template such as `{registration.id}` or `{@organizationId}`, `implies` names the permissions it carries, and `consistent` asks for the consistent answer described above. A service whose functions act on the caller's own organization names that object once, with `resourceId` on `@AuthzResource`: every function is checked on it, whatever its arguments carry, unless it declares its own object, so each function declares only its permission. A service whose functions all need one permission names it once the same way, with `permission` on `@AuthzResource`: deploying, stopping and destroying a workload are all `can_manage_workloads`, so the orchestration declares nothing per function, and a function declaring a permission of its own, on the service or on an interface it extends, keeps it. The permission named is the service's strictest, so a function added later takes it until it declares a weaker one. A function any caller the zone admits may call, such as a listing of the caller's own access, is marked `@AuthzUnchecked` and published with the mark in place of a check, and the service itself answers only what the caller may see or do; a service declaring no resource whose every function answers for the caller alone, such as the caller's own profile or delegates, carries the mark on the interface, which marks every function. A service declaring neither a resource nor the mark is not in the directory and is refused. A function a service inherits, such as `findById` of a CRUD service, is redeclared on the service's interface to carry a check of its own, or marked `@AuthzUnchecked` where the service answers it by what the caller may see, as the application, project and entity definition services' `findById` do. A resource service's function that derives no check and carries neither declaration fails the service's registration, so no function of a resource service goes unchecked by accident.

Every service of the platform is one of six shapes, as the derivation reads it:

::authorization-diagram{view="shapes"}
::

Each pictogram is one service: the function stubs on the left, the permission each needs on its arrow, the object it lands on at the right; the examples under each name the others of its kind.

A service declares roles of its type with `roles` on `@AuthzResource`, each an `@AuthzRole` with an id of the form `<type>.<level>` and the short names of the type's permissions it bundles; a role bundling a permission no function of the type requires fails the model, and a role the model already defines, such as the type's editor, cannot be declared again.

Machines take part the same way: the machines a project's deployment provisions for its workloads are bound as editors of the project when they are created, which is what the sync workload needs to record artifacts and run migrations, and the runtime to publish the project's services.

## The platform's own tier

The platform is a resource too, `platform:kinotic`, the root every organization sits under, and the services published in `system-api` for the platform's operators and machines are checked on it and on what is on it. The platform's own permissions are what running it takes: `can_view_cluster` for the cluster's nodes and the ledger of every watched record, `can_view_members` for the operators, `can_view_machines` and `can_manage_machines` for the platform's machines, `can_view_workloads` and `can_manage_workloads` for the workload records, their deployment, stopping, destruction and deletion, and the restart and removal of a deployed microservice or site, and `can_view_access` and `can_manage_access` for the platform's grants. A read across every organization, such as the listing of organizations, is checked for `organization_can_view` on the platform; a read of one organization, its applications, its projects or its members, is checked on that organization, which a grant made on the platform reaches through the graph. A node is a resource under the platform, `vm_node`: registering one needs `vm_node_can_register_node` on the platform, and a node's heartbeats, status reports and deregistration need `vm_node_can_report` on the node itself.

Three roles come with the platform beside its built-in viewer, editor and admin. `platform.admin` holds everything in the model. `platform.operator` holds the platform's own permissions and the reading permissions of everything on it, so an operator runs the platform's workloads and machines and reads every organization. `platform.support` holds the reading permissions of the platform and of everything on it. A platform operator calling a service of an organization's management plane, such as the logs of a server, is checked on the platform wherever the check names the caller's organization, which an operator has none of; the telemetry services then read any tenant for an operator granted `organization_can_view_telemetry` on the platform, and refuse one granted nothing.

A vm-manager connects as a platform machine granted `vm_node.registrar` on the platform. Its registration of a node writes the node under the platform and binds the machine as the node's `vm_node.agent`, so the machine reports for the node it registered and for no other; a node registering again binds nothing twice, and the finalizer of a deregistration revokes every grant made on the node as it removes the node. When the system server starts and finds the platform has no administrator, it binds every enabled platform operator as `platform.admin` and every enabled platform machine as `vm_node.registrar`, which is how a platform a migration seeds, with its operator and its vm-manager, is run by them; a platform with an administrator is left as it is.

`SystemAccessService`, published in `system-api`, manages the platform's grants: `findRoles` lists the roles a platform grant can name, every role the model defines, `findGrants` the grants made on the platform, `grant` makes one to a platform operator or machine, named as a user subject by its identity id, `revoke` takes it back, and `explain` answers whether an operator or a machine holds one of the platform's own permissions and through which grants. A subject named must be one of the platform's own identities: an organization's member cannot be granted a platform role.

## An application's tier

Every application has an authorization store of its own, named in the engine `kinotic-app-` followed by the 128-bit digest of `<organizationId>.<applicationId>`, which fits the engine's 64-character limit whatever the ids, created with the application and deleted with it and everything in it. Its graph holds the application's end users, tenants and published definitions: a user created in the application is an end user of it and a member of its tenant, a tenant sits under the application, and a definition is placed under the application when it is created and taken out when it is deleted, so a grant made on the application reaches every tenant and every definition. Its model is the same whatever the application publishes: the kernel types, `entity_definition` for a definition's rows, with the permissions the entities repository declares, `can_search`, `can_read`, `can_create`, `can_edit` and `can_delete`, each implying the one before it but `can_create`, `tenant_definition` for those rows within one tenant, and the types of the services registered in the application's scope, which the same worker that keeps the platform's model writes within seconds of a registration, its roles written first so a check against the model never finds a role it grants through behind it. The roles of the rows are the same for every definition, `entity_definition.viewer`, `entity_definition.editor` and `entity_definition.admin`, beside `tenant.admin` for everything in a tenant and `application.admin` for everything in the application; the tenant and the application carry the rows' permissions, so a grant on either reaches every definition's rows below it.

An end user's request to the entities service names the definition it acts on, and is checked on that definition within the caller's tenant, `tenant_definition:<definition id>@<tenant id>`, or on the definition itself for a user with none, for the permission of the rows it needs: reading a row needs `entity_definition_can_read`, listing, counting, querying and searching `entity_definition_can_search`, saving `entity_definition_can_create`, updating `entity_definition_can_edit`, and deleting `entity_definition_can_delete`. The rows within a tenant are reached from a grant on them, from one on the definition in every tenant, and from one on the tenant or the application only for a definition the application holds, so a user naming another application's definition is refused whatever it holds in its own, and a value that can name no resource is refused before the engine is asked. The store holds no default grant, so a user granted nothing is refused everything: an application grants its users what they need, which for an application where every user owns its tenant's data is `tenant.admin` on the user's tenant, made when the user is created. A caller above every application, an organization's member or a platform operator, is checked for the same permission on the definition in the platform's store, where the definition sits under its application, through either repository.

The definition within a tenant is the one object the engine walks without a tuple of its own: the authorizer hands the engine the pair's two edges with the check.

::authorization-diagram{view="pair"}
::

Dashed teal edges exist only for the duration of the check. A grant on the pair, on the definition, on the tenant or on the application all reach the rows; which one the user holds is what the portal's end-user access page shows.

An application's own services take part the same way. The runtime hosting a TypeScript service registers it in the service directory through `ServiceDirectoryService`, published in `app-api` for an organization's runtimes, when its client connects: `register` takes the service's entry, the application, the zone, the version, whether it is advertised, and the definition `kinotic sync` generated from the service's source, with the `@AuthzResource` the class declares and the `@AuthzCheck` of each function the derivation cannot read. It needs `application_can_register_services` on the application, which the declared role `application.runtime` bundles and every runtime machine a deployment provisions holds, and refuses an entry whose zone is not the application's, whose function derives no check and declares none, or whose template names a parameter the function lacks. The platform derives the checks from the definition exactly as it does from a Java interface, stores it in the directory with the application it belongs to, a service published in no namespace in its zone's namespace, `app.acme.crm.ReportService` for a `ReportService` served at `app.acme.crm~ReportService`, and the application's store runs a model carrying the service's type, its permissions and its roles within seconds. A service declaring no resource is registered and listed the same way, and its functions are refused unless the class is marked `@AuthzUnchecked`, which marks every function in the definition `kinotic sync` generates. A request to the service is then checked on the caller's tenant, or on the object a parameter names; a grant reaches an object of the service's own type only once something places the object under a tenant or the application, which nothing does yet, so a service's checks are made on the tenant or the application until then.

::authorization-diagram{view="application"}
::

The same decorators, the same derivation, the same walk; only the store differs. The authorizer never reads the application's code: the entry the runtime registered is the whole contract.

`ApplicationAccessService`, published in `management-api` for an organization's members, manages an application's grants in its store: `findRoles` lists the roles its model defines, `findGrants` the grants that reach a place, each naming where it was made, `grant` makes one to an end user or a machine of the application, named as a user subject by its identity id, on the application, on one of its tenants, on one of its definitions, or on a definition within a tenant, named `<definition id>@<tenant id>`, `revoke` takes it back where it was made, and `explain` answers whether a user holds a permission, named as the model names it, such as `entity_definition_can_read`, and through which grants. The application must belong to the caller's organization, a definition named must be one the application holds, and a subject named must be one of the application's own identities. Viewing an application's access needs `application_can_view_access` and managing it `application_can_manage_access`, permissions of the application in the platform store that its admin, and the organization's, hold.

The platform serves a tenant's users from inside every application's store too. `TenantService` and `TenantMemberService`, published in `app-api` for an application's own pages, act on the caller's tenant: `getTenant`, `rename`, `getSso`, `configureSso` and `removeSso` on the tenant itself, `findMembers`, `inviteMember`, `findPendingInvites`, `cancelInvite` and `removeMember` on its users, `findRoles`, `findGrants`, `grant` and `revoke` on the grants made on it, to its own users only. They are declared on the kernel `tenant` type, so an application's model carries the platform's tenant-typed services beside the application's own, from the kernel model the store is created with: a tenant has `can_view`, `can_edit`, `can_view_members`, `can_manage_members`, `can_view_access` and `can_manage_access`, `tenant.viewer` bundles the viewing ones, `tenant.editor` all of them, and `tenant.admin` those and everything inside the tenant, which is what a customer signing up to an application holds on the tenant it creates. A caller outside every tenant is refused, and a platform service declared on any other type stays out of an application's model.

## In the client

`@kinotic-ai/management-api` mirrors the service as `Kinotic.permissions`, an `IPermissionService` with the same functions over the same models: `RoleDefinition`, `Group`, `Grant`, `Subject` with its `SubjectKind`, `Resource` and `AccessExplanation`, and an application's service as `Kinotic.applicationAccess`, an `IApplicationAccessService` over the same models. The console is built on them. `@kinotic-ai/app-api` mirrors the tenant services for an application's own pages as `Kinotic.tenant`, an `ITenantService`, and `Kinotic.tenantMembers`, an `ITenantMemberService`, over the same models and `Tenant`. `@kinotic-ai/system-api` mirrors the platform's service as `Kinotic.systemAccess`, an `ISystemAccessService` over the same models.

## In the console

Every application and project has an Access page in its sidebar, and every entity definition an Access tab, each with the same panel: the grants made here, the grants inherited from above with a link to where each was made, and a check that explains anyone's access, naming the grants a member or a group holds a permission through. A member who manages the organization's access grants a member or a group one of the roles that reach the node, and revokes a grant where it was made; everyone else sees the panel without its write controls.

Organization settings gains three tabs. Roles lists the built-in roles and the organization's own, with an editor that defines a custom role from the catalog grouped by resource type. Groups lists the groups with their members. Access is the organization's own panel, where a grant reaches everything the organization contains.

A page the gateway refuses, such as a project the member holds nothing on, says so and points back to the list it was opened from, which shows only what the member may see.

An application's Users page in the portal leads to its End-user access page, with a user's drawer opening it for that one user: the grants in the application's own store, on the whole application, on one tenant, on one definition's rows or on those rows in one tenant, chosen at the top, each naming where it was made; a Grant access dialog over the application's users and machines, the same four places, and the roles the application's store defines; and a check that explains whether a user or a machine holds one of the application's permissions there and through which grants. A member who manages the application's access sees the write controls; one who holds nothing on the application sees the page refused.

The system console has an Access page under Members: the grants made on the platform, each to an operator or a machine, a Grant access dialog over the platform's operators and machines and every role the model defines, with the platform's own roles first, and a check that explains whether an operator or a machine holds one of the platform's permissions and through which grants. An operator's drawer on the Users page and a machine's menu on the Machines page open it for that one subject, which is where a vm-manager's machine gets the `vm_node.registrar` role. An operator who manages the platform's access sees the write controls; one who holds a grant that reads it sees the page without them; one who holds neither sees the page refused. A list the gateway refuses, in either console, says so in place of its rows.

## Where the data lives

The engine holds the graph: a role's permissions, a group's members and every grant are relationships in the platform store, an application's end users, tenants and grants in the application's own store, and a check answers from them through the store's generated model. Elasticsearch holds only the names and descriptions of custom roles and groups, in `kinotic_role` and `kinotic_group`, and the record of each store the worker keeps in step, in `kinotic_authz_store`, keyed `platform` for the platform's store and `<organizationId>.<applicationId>` for an application's.
